Environment tiers: a proposal

Proposal, not built. Nothing in this section has been implemented or tested. The stack today has one tier, and the test application sets deployment.environment.name=local.

The question is whether production and non-production telemetry should share one ClickHouse and one Buffer or be stored apart. The proposal is a middle path: one interface for people to query, separate processing and storage per tier, and the environment still carried as an attribute on every record.

  • Prod tier. Its own collectors, Buffer bucket, ingestors and ClickHouse, sized and retained for production needs.
  • Non-prod tier. The same components, smaller, with shorter retention and looser access.
  • One interface. HyperDX, or a query layer in front of both ClickHouse services, so a user searches one place and chooses an environment.
  • Environment attribute. Records keep deployment.environment.name even inside a tier, so filtering by environment works in either design.
ConcernStored togetherStored separately by tier
IsolationA noisy non-prod load or a heavy query shares capacity with production.Non-prod load cannot slow production ingestion or queries.
Failure blast radiusOne ClickHouse or queue problem affects every environment.A problem stays inside one tier.
Retention and accessOne policy per table, so environment-specific rules rely on filters.Each tier has its own retention and its own read-only users.
Cost and operationsOne set of components to run and pay for.Fixed parts such as compute, coordination and collectors are duplicated, and there are two systems to operate.
Cross-environment viewOne query covers everything.Needs the shared interface, and a query across tiers means two queries.

Flush interval

The collector's flush interval sets how many objects it writes to S3. Every flush costs PUT requests, and a short interval at a high event rate becomes the largest S3 cost. The model computes it as follows for the base scenario:

PUTs per month = max(2,592,000 / flush_s,
                     buffer bytes per collector per signal / flush_size_bytes)
                 x collectors x signals x 1.25
GETs per month = objects written x 1 + manifest polls (2,592,000 / poll_s x signals)

The constants are the model's. There are 3 collectors and 2 signals (logs and metrics, since traces had no volume figure), flush_size_bytes is 4 MiB, the factor 1.25 is one data object plus one manifest write per four batches, and the ingestors poll every 1 second. Buffer volume assumes 650 bytes per log and 80 bytes per metric sample in OTLP, compressed 5 times for logs and 4 times for metrics. The prices are $0.0055 per 1,000 PUTs and $0.0044 per 10,000 GETs in Sydney. All of the sizing constants are assumptions, and nothing was measured on AWS.

The local stack flushes every 0.1 s, and measured latency from event to queryable is a median of about 0.6 s for spans, with a 99th percentile of about 4.5 s. A longer flush saves S3 requests and adds roughly its own length to the delay. The 120 s row in the slider shows the other limit: with these assumed volumes, beyond roughly 80 to 90 s the size-based term in the formula is larger than the time-based one, and the request count stops falling with the interval.

Scan guard-rails

Search is the other big lever: a per-scan price turns every broad query into a cost, and self-hosted ClickHouse turns it into load. The general idea is to put limits on what one query or one user can read: a cap on rows or bytes read per query, a cap on query time and time range, and a per-role quota over a period, with a separate role for automated reports. ClickHouse has settings and quotas for these. They were not configured or tested in this stack, so treat this as a design idea and not as a result.
